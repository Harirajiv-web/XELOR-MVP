02 What we copy from them, and what we refuse to

Copy · canary on business metrics

Agoda ships to one cluster first and watches booking rate, latency and exceptions before it goes to every datacentre. Our equivalent watches order-confirm rate, mission-approval latency and RLS denials on one tenant cohort — a release that moves any of them rolls itself back.

Copy · one event spine, many readers

Agoda runs ~2,000 Kafka topics; MakeMyTrip pairs Kafka with Avro and RabbitMQ. The pattern we need is theirs: producers write once, and analytics, integrations and ML each read at their own pace. We feed it from the outbox we already have.

Refuse · a private cloud

Agoda built Fleet, Buckbeak and its own DBaaS over ~1,200 database instances and 2,500 services. That is an answer written by a thousand engineers. Five engineers buy the managed equivalents — EKS, Aurora, MSK, ElastiCache — and spend the saved years on the factory.

Sized for the right problem. Agoda moves 1.8–2 trillion events a day; MakeMyTrip serves 120k orders and 800k notifications a day at 44 ms p50. A manufacturing ERP for Indian MSMEs is a different shape of load — hundreds of plants, thousands of transactions each, every one of which must be correct, tenant-isolated and auditable eight years later. We take their patterns for delivery, eventing and observability, and we size the hardware for correctness rather than for consumer volume.
Researched from: Agoda Engineering on Kafka at 1.8T events/day and on moving 3M CI jobs to Kubernetes · The Pragmatic Engineer, “Inside Agoda’s Private Cloud” (Fleet, Buckbeak on KubeVela, Istio, Ceph, VAST, Iceberg, ~20PB, ~1,200 DB instances, ~2,500 services) · Databricks, “How MakeMyTrip achieved millisecond personalization” (Spark Structured Streaming Real-Time Mode, 44 ms p50, Aerospike, Redis, Kafka).

03 The production topology

One VPC in ap-south-1, three availability zones, two products that stay separate all the way down to their databases — because XELOR runs migrations ONYX has never seen.

EDGE
Route 53 · WAF · CloudFront
DNS, rate limits and rules at the border; static assets and PDFs served from cache.
INGRESS
ALB → EKS ingress
TLS terminated once; host routing splits the two products.
RUNTIME
EKS, three AZs
onyx-api · xelor-api · web · workers · outbox-relay · Gotenberg pool, each scaled on its own signal.
STATE
Managed data plane
Aurora PG (writer + 2 readers) · ElastiCache · MSK · S3 · Keycloak cluster.

Tenancy does not change on the way up

Shared schema with FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY survives into production exactly as written; pods hold per-tenant pooled connections and set app.current_tenant per transaction. The RLS proof that runs in CI runs again in production as a synthetic probe, on a schedule, against a canary tenant — an isolation regression is an alert, not a discovery.

Two products, two deployments, two databases

ONYX and XELOR scale independently and are released independently. XELOR still reads ONYX's records and writes back only through ONYX's own endpoints, now over the mesh with mTLS and its own service identity — so the write path a person uses and the write path an agent uses stay the same path.

04 How a release ships

01
Pull request
Branch protection; no direct push to main.
02
The gates we already run
Build, boundary lint, typecheck, migrate, tests, AI eval and grounding, RLS, permission, naming and manifest proofs.
03
Immutable image
Scanned, signed, SBOM attached, tagged by commit.
04
Staging on the seeded world
The same 157-step demo world, so numbers are comparable release to release.
05
Canary cohort
One tenant cohort, watched on business metrics — not just on CPU.
06
Roll forward or back
Progressive rollout, or automatic rollback with the flag flipped off.

Schema changes are expand-then-contract

Every migration is additive first and destructive only a release later, so the previous image can always still run. Agoda tests every query and index in CI against a snapshot of production shape; we do the same against the seeded world, and no release contains both halves of a rename.

Flags decide, deploys do not

Code ships dark. A feature is enabled per tenant, per plant or per role, which is also how a pilot factory gets something a week before anyone else — and how it is taken away in seconds without a deploy. The AI kill switch is the same mechanism at a coarser grain.

Nothing analytical touches the writer

Reporting reads replicas; anything heavier reads Iceberg. The transactional database is left to do the one job it cannot delegate — being right about stock, ledgers and commitments under concurrency — which is also why an RLS overhead past 15–20% stays a design-review trigger rather than a footnote.

Retention is a legal input, not a storage setting

Statutory records are kept eight years under the MCA rule, on the hash chain that proves they were not edited; personal data carries a DPDP erasure path that must not break that chain. The two are reconciled by erasing the person and keeping the transaction, and the split is decided per table, in code, once.

06 The integration surface

A production ERP is judged by what it connects to. Three surfaces, each with a named owner and a failure mode that is visible rather than silent.

What the plant already runs

XELOR's connectors read SAP, Tally, Odoo, Dynamics and MES/SCADA — change data capture where the system offers it, scheduled extracts where it does not. They are read-only by construction: nothing is ever written back into a customer's existing ERP, and anything XELOR proposes is written through ONYX.

The statutory rails

GST e-invoicing and IRN through a licensed GSP, e-way bills on the same channel, TDS and payroll filings on theirs. Every rate, threshold and window is effective-dated configuration, so a Budget-night change is a data change — never a release.

People and money

WhatsApp through a licensed BSP for approvals and dispatch updates, transactional email and SMS through one provider with delivery receipts, and bank statement import for reconciliation. Every outbound message is logged against the record that caused it.

07 The AI platform in production

The router the MVP already has becomes the control plane: the same four steps, now with money, residency and failure in them.

01
Registry & policy
Closed feature registry, per-tenant opt-out, per-feature risk tier.
02
Budget & residency
Per-tenant token budget checked before spend; PII-bound features pinned to in-country inference.
03
Route with failover
Primary provider, secondary provider, self-hosted vLLM — and the deterministic degraded mode if all three refuse.
04
Ground & verify
Every claim checked against the rows it cites before a human ever sees it.
05
Log & bill
Hash-chained action log plus a cost ledger a tenant can be shown.

Evaluation does not stop at the merge

The golden-set and grounding gates keep blocking CI. Production adds shadow evaluation — a candidate model answers the same questions as the live one without reaching a user — plus drift alarms on refusal rate, grounding failures and cost per decision. A provider is promoted on evidence, not on a release note.

Personal data does not leave the country

DPDP is a routing rule, not a policy document: features that can carry personal data are pinned to self-hosted inference inside ap-south-1, and the router refuses to send them anywhere else. Egress is enforced where the call is made, so no module can opt out of it.

08 The agents at scale

Durable, not merely retried

Node claims, heartbeats and bounded attempts already exist. Production runs them on a dedicated worker pool with per-tenant concurrency caps, so one plant's mission storm cannot starve another's, and any run can be replayed from its journal.

Approvals have a clock

A run waiting on a person is a run costing money. Approvals carry an SLA, escalate up the role chain, and reach the approver where they are — push, email, and WhatsApp through a licensed BSP.

Three kill switches

Per feature, per tenant, and global. Each one drops the affected feature to its declared degraded mode rather than to an error, so the factory keeps running while the model is off.

10 Capacity, cost and how it grows

The unit that scales

Tenants share one cluster and one database until a plant's volume or its contract says otherwise; then it moves to its own Aurora instance without changing a line, because every query is already tenant-scoped. Nothing in the code knows which arrangement it is in.

Where the money goes

Aurora and MSK dominate the bill long before compute does, and GPU inference dominates both if it is used carelessly — which is why the token budget sits in the router and the cost per decision is a monitored metric rather than a month-end surprise.

What triggers the next step

Three signals, each with a number: RLS overhead past 20%, replica lag past the reporting SLA, or a single tenant past a quarter of cluster throughput. Any one of them opens a design review — the same rule the platform already applies in development.

11 Running it, and putting a plant on it

Operations

A severity ladder with a named on-call rota, a runbook per alert that has fired at least once, a public status page, and a blameless review inside five working days of any Sev-1. Support tiers are contractual: response times by plan, and a named engineer for pilot accounts.

Putting a plant live

Tenant and plant provisioned from a template, masters imported from Tally or spreadsheets through the data-import module with a dry run before commit, roles mapped to the people who actually sign, then a fortnight running in parallel with the paper before cutover — and the AI features switched on last, one at a time.

One thing does not scale away. Every service above is replaceable — the model, the queue, the cloud. The invariant is not: AI explains, it never decides. Nothing reaches a customer, a supplier, a ledger or a machine without a person approving it, and every approval stays reconstructable — the cited rows, the model call, the permission held, and the hash chain proving none of it was edited since.
