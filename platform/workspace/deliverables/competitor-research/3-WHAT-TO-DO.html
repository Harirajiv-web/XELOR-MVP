What to change, in what order, and what each change buys us

Twelve changes taken from what these companies have already worked out. Ordered by how much they help against how hard they are. Almost none of them is engineering work — which is the whole point.

17 August 2026 12 actions · 3 phases Nothing here needs money we do not have

1The one thing, if you only do one

Get one real factory running XELOR, and get one named person there to say a sentence about it.

Every gap in Document 2 traces back to this. Workroom is a six-person company that beat us on nothing except having customers. Seewise’s single strongest asset is eight named people at eight named companies saying it worked.

We cannot out-build our way past this, and we cannot out-argue it. One live plant closes most of the gap. Everything else in this document is either a way to get that plant, or a way to make the most of it once we have it.

2Phase one — this month

Everything here is words and decisions. No engineering. All of it can be done alongside classes.

Action 1 — Rename the nine after the jobs they do

What to change. A buyer sees SPAR, KILN, AXLE, MICA, RASP, HEXA, ONYX. They mean nothing outside our team. Seewise names theirs after the job: furnace monitoring, weighbridge automation, truck loading.

Do this: lead with the job, keep the codename in small print for ourselves.

What it buys us. A plant manager understands the product in five seconds instead of needing us to explain it. Costs half a day. This is the cheapest large improvement available to us.

Action 2 — Rewrite the opening line

Ours says what we are. Theirs say what the buyer gets.

Today

“An AI operating system for the factory floor.”

“Operating system” is a technical term. It describes us, not them.

Change to

“It works out what your factory should do next — and asks you before it does it.”

No technical word. Benefit and safety in one breath. It is already our argument, just buried.

What it buys us. The first sentence is the only one most people read. Ours currently spends it on ourselves.

Action 3 — Design the pilot offer

Copy Seewise’s structure, change the length. Theirs is 14 days because a camera can be installed in an afternoon. Ours touches orders and stock, so 21 days is honest.

The offer should read:

  • 21 days. One order followed all the way through, start to finish.
  • We visit first, for free, and write down what we find — that document is worth having even if they never buy.
  • Runs alongside what they use today. Tally stays. Nothing is switched off. Nothing is at risk.
  • One measure, agreed in writing before we start (see Action 8).
  • Paid, but small. Workroom’s founder learned the trade on paid project work. A free pilot proves curiosity; a paid one proves intent.

What it buys us. Today we ask a factory owner to believe a demo. This asks them to approve three weeks with no disruption — a decision a plant manager can make alone. It converts our biggest weakness (no proof) into a first step someone can actually say yes to.

Action 7 — Ask CoSol for three introductions

This is the asset the other two companies do not have and are actively paying to build. Seewise is hiring a channel partner manager. Workroom’s founder spent years doing VR projects inside factories to earn the access. We are already inside a Cisco Gold Partner that sells into these plants.

The ask is small and specific: three introductions to plant managers in Peenya for a free operations review. Not a sale. A review.

What it buys us. This is the shortest path to Action 1’s “one real factory”. Every other route — cold calls, ads, events — costs money and months that we do not have.

3Phase two — months two and three

Action 5 — Build five pages named after real jobs

Seewise has seven, named by trade. Ours should be named for what our first buyers actually struggle with:

Can we take this order?

Answering a customer’s date question in minutes instead of half a day.

What is actually in the stores?

Knowing what is on the shelf without a physical count.

Where is that order right now?

Following one job through the plant.

Getting through an audit

Turning two weeks of hunting into two days.

Who is on what today?

Assigning people by skill instead of by memory.

Use Seewise’s page shape, which works: the problem stated bluntly → how it works in four steps → what it does, with numbers → how it compares to doing nothing → what you get → book a review.

What it buys us. A buyer recognises their own problem instead of decoding a platform.

Action 6 — Put up a real website

We currently have no address a stranger can arrive at. Seewise has ninety pages. Workroom has five. We have a deck that only works when one of us is in the room.

It does not need to be large. Workroom won Apollo Tyres with five pages. The minimum is: home, the five job pages from Action 5, what we cannot do yet, and a way to book a review.

Action 8 — Agree the success test before the pilot starts

One page, signed before day one, saying: here is the number today, here is what it must reach in 21 days, here is who measures it.

Seewise’s founder put the reason plainly: “Once we presented the data showing a clear return on investment, even the staunchest sceptics started to notice.” You cannot present that data unless you wrote down the starting number before you began.

What it buys us. Without it, a successful pilot ends in a vague good feeling. With it, it ends in a number we can publish — which is exactly what we lack.

Action 9 — Fix the case-study template now, not later

Copy Seewise’s six blocks and write the blank form before the pilot finishes:

  1. Headline number, industry, type of job
  2. The problem — bullets on what was going wrong
  3. What we switched on
  4. How it works — step by step
  5. The numbers that changed
  6. What it meant for the business

And copy their anonymising trick: publish it as “a pump manufacturer in Peenya”. Our first customer will be nervous about being named; this way we still get to publish the detail.

Action 10 — Make the honesty page a feature, not a footnote

Neither competitor publishes what they cannot do. We already have that list. Right now it is slide twelve of a deck. It should be a page on the website with a plain title: “What XELOR does not do yet”.

In a market where every company claims AI, being the one that publishes its own gaps is a real position — and it costs nothing because the work is already done.

4Phase three — month four onward

Action 11 — Write for factory people

Seewise has 56 articles: cutting energy costs in foundries, what changes when AI goes live on a shop floor, why a pilot is different from production. That is how strangers find them with no salesperson involved.

Realistic for us: one article a fortnight. Write what we actually learn in the first pilot. “What we found in our first Peenya factory” is a better article than anything we could invent.

This is slow. Start it now anyway, because it only pays back after several months and the clock has already started.

Action 12 — Turn CoSol into a channel, not just an introduction

Seewise is hiring a channel partner manager. That is a salary and six months of recruiting to build something we already sit inside.

The step up from Action 7 is a proper arrangement: CoSol’s salespeople carry XELOR into their existing conversations, with a commission and a one-page explainer they can leave behind. One partner selling for us is worth more than any advertising we could afford.

Action 4 — Publish how a rollout actually goes

Seewise publishes four steps with real numbers, including admitting the system starts at 60% accuracy before reaching 98%. The admission is what makes everything else believable.

Ours would be: week one we connect and watch, week two we run one order alongside your existing way, week three you compare the two. And the honest line: “in week one it will get things wrong, and we will show you every one of them.”

6What we should not copy

Copying the wrong thing costs as much as copying nothing.

Do not copy Workroom’s empty solution pages

Feature lists with no proof. They can afford that because their home page carries four customer names. We cannot — with nothing to show, feature lists read as claims.

Do not copy the unverified big numbers

“42,000 hours saved” with no source is fine when you have 25 clients. From us it is a liability, and it would destroy the one advantage we have — being the honest one.

Do not chase money first

Workroom raised on a Forbes listing and four customers. We have neither yet. Seewise reached 42 people with no investors at all. Proof first. The money follows it, not the other way round.

Do not narrow the product to match them

Our breadth is real and it is our advantage. The answer to Seewise is not to build cameras; it is to be the system that everything else reports into.
