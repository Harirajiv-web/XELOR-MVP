# Network questions and findings

Archived on 22 September 2026. This note records the buyer-validation approach and previously researched findings. It introduces no new ranking and is not a final investment recommendation. No customer outreach has been conducted. Best-case access to capital, technical partners and industry connections remains an assumption, not a confirmed commitment.

The most useful question to put through the user's network is:

> Which product do you regularly import, want manufactured locally, and have a practical route to qualifying and buying from a new Indian supplier?

The useful answer identifies a product and a purchasing process. Connect with the engineers who approve it and the procurement team that can issue paid development and production orders.

## Eight questions for the buyer

1. **What exactly should we make?** Obtain the part number, drawing or specification, application, package or machine compatibility, and current source country. Establish whether the buyer wants an interchangeable replacement, a second source or a newly designed product.

2. **What quantities are actually purchased?** Ask for annual consumption, order sizes, delivery frequency and expected demand over the product programme. Separate historical purchases, forecasts and binding commitments. Identify what share could realistically go to a new supplier.

3. **Why would you add or change a supplier?** Establish the problem: delivery time, price, minimum order size, service, quality, customisation or supply continuity. Ask what the incumbent already does well and what an Indian supplier must improve.

4. **What delivered price would make the switch worthwhile?** Request the current landed-cost basis and target purchase price, including relevant freight, duties, testing and warranty responsibilities. Clarify tooling charges, payment terms and price adjustments. A broad import-market value cannot answer this question.

5. **Who approves the product, and against which tests?** Identify engineering, quality and procurement decision-makers. Obtain acceptance criteria, reliability requirements, audit expectations, required documentation and an indicative qualification schedule. Clarify whether the equipment OEM or overseas parent must also approve.

6. **Will you pay for development and qualification?** Discuss a paid sample or pilot order, sample quantities, tooling ownership and responsibility for laboratory costs. Agree milestones and what happens if samples need redesign. Willingness to evaluate free samples is a weaker signal.

7. **What happens after the product passes?** Ask whether approval leads to a supplier nomination, trial production order, framework agreement or only permission to quote. Seek a written commercial path with quantities and release conditions; technical approval alone does not guarantee purchases.

8. **How long will this product remain relevant?** Ask about remaining programme life, technology changes, redesigns and replacement generations. Establish how customer forecasts and engineering changes will be communicated, and who pays for obsolete tooling or inventory.

## Product families retained for buyer discussions

These seven families are a discussion shortlist, not an order of preference:

- **Test boards and sockets:** load boards, burn-in boards and package-specific sockets for chipmakers, test companies and packaging plants. Separate local assembly from ownership of contact technology and advanced board fabrication.
- **Leadframes:** stamped or etched, plated structures used inside specified chip packages. Package geometry, volumes and reliability approval determine the opportunity; GPU packaging growth does not automatically create leadframe demand.
- **Advanced substrates:** the specialised base connecting chips to the wider system. Organic substrates and glass/interposer products require different process choices and technical partners; neither is interchangeable with ordinary PCB assembly.
- **Packaging and test automation:** repeatable handling, sorting, test-loading or tape-and-reel machines for semiconductor production lines. Customer throughput, damage prevention and uptime requirements define the first machine.
- **Inspection equipment:** focused inspection of leadframes, bonds, package surfaces, contacts or tape pockets. Buyers need demonstrated defect detection and controlled false rejects, not merely a camera with an AI label.
- **Vacuum and process subsystems:** complete qualified chamber or load-lock assemblies with the required interfaces and controls. Supplying exported equipment is distinct from replacing imports used in Indian fabs.
- **Carriers and specialised trays:** semiconductor handling and transport products with suitable cleanliness, dimensional stability and automation compatibility. Ordinary ESD storage products do not automatically meet wafer or chip-package requirements.

## Turning introductions into a reviewable business case

Government and industry introductions can help reach sourcing teams, technology owners, laboratories and relevant programme administrators. They do not establish a technology licence, subsidy entitlement or customer nomination. A technology-transfer agreement must specify manufacturing rights, usable process documentation, training, qualification support, territory, improvements and commercial terms. A collaboration announcement alone proves none of those rights for this company.

The preferred progression is a defined customer requirement, a paid development or qualification order, documented test acceptance, and repeat production purchase orders. Confirm customer quantities and prices, obtain machine and process quotations, and test the economics before committing major factory expenditure. Product shortages strengthen the reason to investigate; they do not remove qualification, execution or commercial risk.
