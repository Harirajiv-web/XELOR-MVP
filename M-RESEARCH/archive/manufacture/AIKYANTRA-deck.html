Two small parts, made in India

AIKYANTRA

We want to make two things in Bengaluru: the charging socket on electric vehicles, and the metal parts that stop machines from overheating.

Both are ordinary-looking parts. Both are bought from other countries today. And for both, the government has now told Indian vehicle makers that these parts have to be made here. This deck explains what they are, why now, and exactly how we would do it.

Idea one · what we would make

The socket on the vehicle.

Think of charging your phone. The charger has a pin. The phone has a hole. An electric bus works the same way: the charging gun is the pin, and the socket built into the bus is the hole. We make the hole.

There are two kinds
  • The slow one. Used overnight, like charging your phone while you sleep. Small pins.
  • The fast one. Fills a bus battery in a short break, so far more electricity has to pass through. It has two extra thick pins for that.
  • Buses need both. The fast one is worth roughly what a good phone costs.
Why it is not easy to make
  • It gets hot. A lot of electricity through a small metal pin creates heat. The pin has to stay cooler than boiling water, or it damages the vehicle.
  • It gets used roughly. It must survive being plugged in and pulled out about ten thousand times without going loose.
  • It lives outdoors. Buses are washed with water jets and driven through rain, dust and heat. Nothing may get inside.
And that is exactly why we want it

If a part were easy, a hundred workshops would already make it and the price would be pushed to nothing.

This one is hard, so very few make it. India makes the cable and the charging gun well. The socket still comes from abroad — and the one skill holding us back is the silver coating on the pins. That can be learnt.

Idea one · why now

Why this is the right moment.

Three things have come together: a new rule that forces this part to be made in India, buyers sitting close to us, and hardly anyone here who makes it.

24.5 lakhElectric vehicles were sold in India last year. Every single one of them needs a socket.
More than halfOf these sockets are still bought from other countries.
4,500Electric buses are coming to Bengaluru alone, and each one needs two sockets.
A handfulOf Indian companies actually make this part today.
1 · A rule now protects us

The government helps pay for electric buses. In return it says: certain parts of that bus must be made in India.

The charging socket is on that list.

This matters because it is a rule, not a grant. Nobody has to release money for it to apply — the bus maker simply has to buy the part from an Indian supplier.

2 · The buyers are our neighbours

Only a handful of companies build electric buses in India. Two of them are close to us: Tata at Dharwad, and Ashok Leyland at Hosur, less than an hour from Bengaluru.

Few buyers sounds risky, but for a small factory it is easier — we can visit all of them, and each one buys the same part again and again.

3 · Hardly anyone makes it here

India is good at making the cable and the charging gun. The socket is the piece still coming from Germany, Japan, the US and China.

The big foreign companies sell in India, but they do not build here. So the moment a bus maker needs an Indian supplier, there are very few names to call.

What it sells forOne fast socket for a bus is worth about the price of a good phone. A scooter socket is worth a few hundred rupees — which is why we start with buses.
Why the customer staysChanging this part means testing the whole vehicle again. So once we are approved, they keep buying from us for years.
The honest catchIt takes two to three years of testing and approvals before the first steady order. This is a patient business.
What we can do meanwhileSell simpler versions and spare sockets to bus depots while the big approvals are going on.

Where the materials come from
  • The pins — cut and shaped by Indian metal press workshops, from our drawing.
  • The silver coating — done by an outside specialist at first. Later we bring it in-house, because it is the hardest and most valuable step.
  • The plastic — a standard engineering plastic, sold openly in India.
  • Sensor and lock motor — small bought-in items, as every maker does.
  • The mould — made once by a Bengaluru tool room, then used for thousands of parts.
How the orders come
  • First, samples. We make ten by hand. No big factory needed for this.
  • Then, government testing. A national lab heats it, freezes it, sprays water on it and plugs it in thousands of times. Passing this is what makes us a real supplier.
  • Then, a factory visit. The bus maker inspects how we work and keep records.
  • Then, a trial batch. They measure our first pieces closely and approve them.
  • Then regular orders — plus spare sockets for depots, which repeat on their own.
How we start, in three sizes

Small — about ₹1.5 to 3 crore. We buy the pins and bodies ready-made and only assemble, seal and test. We start earning and learning quickly.

Full — about ₹8 to 15 crore. Our own mould, our own assembly line and test room. This is the version bus makers approve.

Later — our own coating line. The step India is missing. Then other companies come to us for it too, not just our own product.

Idea two · the cooling business

A company that keeps machines cool.

Start with something everyone has felt: a phone gets warm when you use it heavily. Every machine that uses electricity does the same thing — it turns some of that electricity into heat. The bigger the machine, the bigger the heat.

First, why fans stopped working

A laptop has a fan. That is enough for a laptop.

But today's computer chips are far more powerful. One chip can give off as much heat as a room heater — from a piece the size of a postcard. A fan blowing air over it is like blowing on hot tea: it helps a little, and then it stops helping.

So the world switched to liquid

Water carries heat away far better than air. That is why a car engine has a radiator with coolant running through it, not just a fan.

Computers, batteries and chargers are now cooled the same way: liquid is carried right up to the hot part, picks up the heat, and takes it outside.

Where our factory fits in

We do not make the chip, the battery or the pump. Those are somebody else's business.

We make the metal that carries the liquid: the flat plate that sits on the hot part, the pipes, and the block that splits one pipe into many.

It is careful, accurate metalwork that must never leak. That is the whole business.

Idea two · exactly what we do

Four jobs, done properly.

This factory is not complicated. Every part we make is the same four jobs, done accurately every time. Learn them once, and the same workshop can serve buses, cars, chargers, tractors and computer rooms.

Job 01

Cut and bend the pipe

Metal pipe is cut to length and bent to the exact shape the customer has drawn.

Pipe bending machine
Job 02

Drill the block

A solid block of metal is drilled and shaped so every pipe fits into it perfectly.

Computer-controlled machines
Job 03

Join it together

The joints are welded so they are as strong as the metal around them.

Trained welders
Job 04

Prove it never leaks

A gas is pushed inside and a machine sniffs for a leak far too small for the eye to see. Every single piece is checked.

This is what we are really selling
Where the materials come from
  • Aluminium and steel pipe and plate — ordinary sizes, bought from Indian suppliers.
  • Fittings and rubber hoses — bought in, from the list the customer approves.
  • No rare material, no import permission, no chemicals. That simplicity is a strength.
How the orders come
  • We do not go to the bus companies. We go to the companies that make the cooling system for them.
  • Those companies already buy these exact parts from outside, so we are not asking them to change how they work.
  • They take under a year to approve a new supplier, where a vehicle maker takes two.
  • Then it is simple: make it to their drawing, deliver on time, never leak. That is how the next order comes.
Three ways to start

Small — ₹1 to 1.5 crore. Bending pipes and making brackets for others. Money starts coming in within a year, though the profit per piece is small.

Proper — ₹2.5 to 4 crore. We make the junction blocks and pipe sets ourselves and do our own leak testing. This is the one I would choose.

Bigger, later — ₹8 to 15 crore. The plates that touch the hot part. Harder to make, and they sell to vehicles, batteries and computer rooms alike.

Why customers stayA leak in the field is a disaster for them. Once a supplier has proved it never leaks, they stop looking elsewhere.
What changes between industriesOnly the metal, and how clean it has to be. The machines and the skills stay the same.
The honest catchEach piece earns little. This business makes money by repeating well, not by selling one expensive part.

The whole point of this page

Buses alone are too small a market to build a company on.

But the same workshop, the same machines and the same people can serve all six. That is what turns a small parts shop into a real business.

One rule we will follow

One industry at a time. Each new one brings fresh approvals and paperwork, not just a fresh customer. Trying to serve all six in year one is how small factories fail.

Where scooters fit in

They don't, and it is better to say so. Scooter and auto batteries are cooled by plain air, so India's biggest electric segment buys nothing from this factory.

What the building must have
  • Electricity — a three-phase connection of about 100 to 150 kVA, and a backup generator so a power cut does not spoil a weld halfway through.
  • Height and a hoist — about 6 metres of clear height and a small overhead crane, because metal blocks and finished assemblies are heavy.
  • Compressed air — used by almost every machine and for cleaning.
  • Clean water — a small purifying setup for the final wash, so nothing is left inside the pipes.
  • A proper floor — level and strong enough for machines that must not shake.
  • No chemicals, no plating — which keeps the pollution clearance simple and the neighbours happy.
Registrations and paperwork
  • Company registration, GST, and small-enterprise (Udyam) registration — the last one legally requires customers to pay us within 45 days.
  • Factory licence and fire clearance.
  • Pollution board consent — the simple category, since we use no chemicals.
  • Provident fund and employee insurance registration.
  • Insurance for fire, machine breakdown and product liability.
  • ISO 9001 in the first year — the basic quality certificate every customer asks to see. The stricter vehicle-industry certificate comes later.
Where the money goes
  • Machines — ₹1.6 to 2.2 crore
  • Testing and measuring equipment — ₹50 to 80 lakh. This is what customers are really paying for.
  • Shed deposit, wiring, air, crane, fit-out — ₹30 to 50 lakh
  • First year's salaries and material money — ₹60 lakh to ₹1 crore
  • Total: roughly ₹3 to 4.5 crore to be properly running, with money left to survive slow payments.
If we build the socket factory instead
  • Smaller shed — 3,000 to 4,000 sq ft is enough, because the parts are small.
  • Fewer people — about 12 to 15, but more of them on assembly and electrical testing than on welding.
  • One rare specialist — someone who understands metal coating. Without that person, this business does not work.
  • Cleaner room — small parts, fine seals, so dust matters even more.
  • The plastic moulding is done outside at first, so we do not buy that machinery on day one.
Hire this person firstThe production head. Everything else — machines, customers, approvals — is chosen better once that person is in the room.
Doubling output is cheapA second shift adds operators, not machines. Capacity can nearly double for the cost of a few more salaries.
Where the shed should beJust outside Bengaluru — Kolar, Malur, Harohalli or Tumakuru. Rent is lower, and the state gives a bigger subsidy there than inside the city.
Keep this much asideCustomers pay in 60 to 120 days. We need three to four months of salaries and material money sitting in the bank, separate from the machines.
