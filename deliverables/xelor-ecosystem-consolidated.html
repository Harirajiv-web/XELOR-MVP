Where all eighteen went.

Each merge is justified by something concrete the parts share — the same master record, the same device on the wall, the same person signing the cheque. Merges made only because two names sound similar are not merges; they are a bigger menu with fewer headings.

Plant Operations

3 → 1
  • Connected Factory
  • Maintenance & Reliability
  • Energy & Utilities

Why they mergeAll three hang off one equipment register and one gateway on the wall. A machine event, a repair history and a kilowatt reading are three readings of the same asset. Sold separately, the customer pays to survey and wire the same plant three times.

Buyer: plant head or works manager. Stays in XELOR: routine operator transactions and work-order booking. 14 capabilities — maintenance is the readiest part of the whole ring, energy the least.

Quality, Safety & Compliance

2 → 1
  • Quality & Audit
  • Safety & Permits

Why they mergeOne corrective-action engine. A defect and a near-miss run an identical loop — raise, contain, investigate, act, evidence, audit. Both need controlled documents, training records and an audit trail. Keeping them apart means building that engine twice and auditing it twice.

Buyer: QA and EHS, usually one department; ISO 9001 and 45001 are audited together. Stays in XELOR: routine receipt and in-process inspection. 10 capabilities.

Warehouse & Dispatch

Why they mergeOne handling unit and one scanner in one hand. Picking and packing run straight into loading the truck — splitting them cuts a single continuous physical motion in half and asks the same storeman to carry two apps.

Buyer: stores or logistics manager. Stays in XELOR: the stock ledger remains the authoritative inventory record; routine dispatch stays core fulfilment. 9 capabilities — the least-built bundle on the ring.

What consolidating does not change.

It is packaging, not code

No capability was merged out of existence, renamed away or quietly promoted from proposed to built. The readiness labels travel with each capability into its new package, and most of this ring is still proposed work.

The ERP core keeps its boundary

Everyday transactions, operator actions, budget control, internal supplier history and permissions remain in XELOR whichever packages a customer buys. Core finance and operator work must not move behind a paid add-on.

Two cross-package couplings to honour

Permit-to-work sits in Compliance but gates maintenance jobs in Plant Operations. The connector runtime sits in the platform but is operated by Services. Both need a defined interface, not a copy on each side.

Nothing here is a commitment to build

This regroups a research catalogue. Six packages is a cheaper story to tell than eighteen, but it is the same amount of engineering underneath, and most of it has not started.

Basis. Groupings were drawn from the 94 capability records in the original ecosystem map, including each capability's state — extend an existing foundation, build new, partner-delivered or service-delivered. The five-into-one services merge is the clearest case: every one of those twenty-one capabilities is marked delivery or partner except the connector runtime.

Not established here. Pricing, licence packaging, per-package margins, customer demand for any bundle, and whether a buyer will purchase a merged package at a higher price than a single component. Those are commercial questions this map does not answer.

A packaging proposal derived from the existing XELOR ecosystem map and the manufacturing research behind it. Merged packages still require completion and validation; grouping offerings together does not imply they are built, integrated or ready to deploy.
